History of the World Wide Web

From a hypertext proposal at CERN to a platform used by billions - how linked documents became the dominant way humans read, publish, and shop.

Invention Public Launch and Growth Standardization Web 2.0 and Modern Web

Invention and First Deployment (1989-1991)

The Web began as an internal fix for a very specific problem: CERN researchers kept losing track of each other's documents.

March 1989 - Tim Berners-Lee Proposes the Web

Berners-Lee submitted "Information Management: A Proposal" to CERN management, describing a hypertext system for linking documents across the lab.

Why it mattered: the proposal introduced hypertext, linked documents, and a unified information space - the conceptual seed of the modern Web.

Source: W3C - A Short History of the Web

October 1990 - First Web Server and Browser Built

Berners-Lee built the first web server and the first browser/editor, "WorldWideWeb," on a NeXT computer at CERN.

Why it mattered: this proved the whole stack - HTTP, HTML, URLs, and a working client and server - was technically feasible, not just a proposal on paper.

Source: Wikipedia - WorldWideWeb

December 20, 1990 - First Website Goes Live

The first website, info.cern.ch, was published, describing the Web project itself and how to use it.

Why it mattered: it marked the birth of the Web as a publishable medium - a demonstration, not just a description, of linked information.

Source: CERN - The Birth of the Web

November 1991 - First Webcam: The Trojan Room Coffee Pot

Researchers at Cambridge University pointed a camera at a coffee pot and connected the feed to the Web, so nobody had to walk down the hall for a disappointing empty pot.

Why it mattered: it was a small, funny demonstration that the Web could carry live, real-time media - an early hint of what "always-on" content would become.

Source: Wikipedia - Trojan Room Coffee Pot

Public Launch and Early Growth (1991-1994)

Once the Web left CERN, growth depended on two things: making it free, and making it visual.

August 6, 1991 - Web Opened to the Public

Berners-Lee posted a summary of the Web project to the alt.hypertext newsgroup and made the files available by FTP, inviting the wider Internet to use it.

Why it mattered: this is the Web's public debut beyond high-energy physics - the moment it became available to anyone online.

Source: W3C blog

February-November 1993 - NCSA Mosaic Browser Released

NCSA released Mosaic, created by Marc Andreessen and Eric Bina - first for Unix, then for Macintosh and Windows by November - as the first widely used browser to show images inline with text.

Why it mattered: Mosaic made the Web look like a modern, visual medium instead of a text-only academic tool, triggering an explosion in servers, sites, and everyday users.

Source: Wikipedia - NCSA Mosaic

April 30, 1993 - CERN Releases Web Software into the Public Domain

CERN announced that the Web's browser and server software would be royalty-free, available to anyone, forever.

Why it mattered: this kept the Web open and non-proprietary, preventing it from fragmenting into competing, incompatible systems owned by different companies.

Source: History.com

July 5, 1994 - Amazon Founded

Jeff Bezos founded an online bookstore, originally called "Cadabra," in Bellevue, Washington; it went public on the Web the following year.

Why it mattered: Amazon showed the Web could support secure transactions and logistics at scale, laying the groundwork for online retail as we know it.

Source: History.com

Standardization and the Browser Wars (1994-2001)

Success created a new problem: competing browsers wanted to control the Web's future. Someone had to referee.

October 1994 - World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) Founded

Berners-Lee founded the W3C at MIT, working with CERN, DARPA, and the European Commission to develop open Web standards.

Why it mattered: the W3C kept HTML, CSS, and HTTP interoperable across competing browsers, preventing the Web from splitting into vendor-locked versions.

Source: W3C - About / History

December 1994 - Netscape Navigator 1.0 Released

Netscape Communications, founded by Marc Andreessen and Jim Clark, shipped Navigator - faster and more reliable than Mosaic, and the first mainstream browser with SSL encryption.

Why it mattered: Navigator popularized the Web for everyday users and made secure e-commerce technically possible for the first time.

Source: Computer History Museum

August 1995 - Microsoft Internet Explorer 1.0 Released

Microsoft shipped Internet Explorer 1.0, built on licensed Mosaic code, as an add-on to Windows 95 Plus!, entering the browser market directly against Netscape.

Why it mattered: this ignited the first "browser war," which Microsoft eventually won by bundling IE with Windows - reshaping the browser market for a decade.

Source: Wikipedia - Browser Wars

September 4, 1998 - Google Incorporated

Larry Page and Sergey Brin formally incorporated Google, building on the PageRank search algorithm they had developed as Stanford students.

Why it mattered: Google's search became the dominant way people found anything on the Web, and its advertising model would go on to fund much of the free Web.

Source: Google - Our Story

January 15, 2001 - Wikipedia Launched

Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger launched Wikipedia, a free encyclopedia that anyone could edit.

Why it mattered: Wikipedia proved mass, volunteer collaboration could produce a reference work used and trusted by hundreds of millions of people.

Source: Wikipedia - History of Wikipedia

Web 2.0 and the Modern Web (2004-Present)

The last turning points shifted the Web from something you read to something you participate in - and eventually, carry in your pocket.

February 4, 2004 - Facebook Launched

Mark Zuckerberg, with Harvard classmates Eduardo Saverin, Dustin Moskovitz, Chris Hughes, and Andrew McCollum, launched "TheFacebook" as a Harvard-only social network.

Why it mattered: Facebook popularized social networking and user-generated content, defining what people now call "Web 2.0."

Source: History.com

February 14, 2005 - YouTube Founded

Former PayPal employees Chad Hurley, Steve Chen, and Jawed Karim registered the YouTube domain and began building a video-sharing platform.

Why it mattered: YouTube made video publishing accessible to anyone, reshaping media and entertainment on the Web.

Source: Wikipedia - History of YouTube

January 9, 2007 - iPhone Unveiled

Steve Jobs announced the first iPhone at Macworld, combining a phone, an iPod, and "a breakthrough Internet communications device" with real desktop-class web browsing.

Why it mattered: the iPhone shifted the center of gravity of the Web from desktops to smartphones, kicking off the mobile-first era of the Internet.

Source: Apple Newsroom

January 22, 2008 - HTML5 First Public Working Draft Published

The W3C and WHATWG published the first official working draft of HTML5, unifying and extending HTML, CSS, and DOM APIs.

Why it mattered: HTML5 introduced native video, audio, and offline storage, reducing reliance on plugins like Flash and enabling the richer web applications we use today.

Source: WHATWG blog
