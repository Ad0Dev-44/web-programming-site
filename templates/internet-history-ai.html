History of the Internet

How a Cold War-era research experiment in packet switching grew, wire by wire and protocol by protocol, into the network of networks.

Early Foundations Protocol Unification Infrastructure Expansion Broadband and Mobile Growth The Contemporary Internet

Early Foundations (1960s-1970s)

Before there was an "Internet," there was a single experimental network, funded by the U.S. Department of Defense, trying to prove that separate computers could talk to each other at all.

October 29, 1969 - First ARPANET Message

A UCLA student programmer, Charley Kline, tried to log into a computer at the Stanford Research Institute over ARPANET. The system crashed after transmitting just two letters, "LO," of the intended word "LOGIN."

Why it mattered: it was the first proof that geographically separated computers could exchange data over a packet-switched network - the seed of everything that followed.

Source: Computer History Museum

1971 - First Networked Email

Ray Tomlinson, working at Bolt, Beranek and Newman, sent the first message between two separate host machines on ARPANET, and picked the @ symbol to separate a user's name from the name of their computer.

Why it mattered: email became the network's first true "killer app," turning ARPANET from a research curiosity into a communication tool people actually wanted to use - and the user@host convention is still with us.

Source: Wikipedia - Ray Tomlinson

1973 - First International ARPANET Connection

ARPANET stretched across the Atlantic for the first time, linking University College London with the Royal Radar Establishment in Norway.

Why it mattered: it showed that packet switching could survive contact with different countries' telecommunications systems, hinting at a genuinely global network.

Source: Council on Foreign Relations

Protocol Unification (1980s)

A network is only useful if every machine on it speaks the same language. The 1980s were about agreeing - by force, in one case - on that shared language.

January 1, 1983 - TCP/IP "Flag Day" on ARPANET

On a single coordinated date, all roughly 400 hosts on ARPANET switched over from the older Network Control Program to the TCP/IP protocol suite designed by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn.

Why it mattered: TCP/IP let independent networks interconnect as one system. Many historians mark this "flag day" as the operational birth of the modern Internet - a true network of networks.

Source: Internet Society

November 1983 - DNS Invented

Paul Mockapetris, working at USC's Information Sciences Institute, published the specifications for the Domain Name System.

Why it mattered: DNS replaced a single manually-updated text file of every host on the network with a distributed, scalable naming system - without it, the Internet could never have grown past a few hundred machines.

Source: History of DNS

Infrastructure Expansion (1980s-1990s)

With a common protocol settled, the remaining question was who would build - and eventually let go of - the physical backbone carrying all that traffic.

1986 - NSFNET Backbone Launched

The U.S. National Science Foundation funded NSFNET, a 56 Kbps backbone connecting supercomputing centers and regional research networks, operated by Merit Network and partners.

Why it mattered: NSFNET became the de facto U.S. Internet backbone, scaling the network from thousands to millions of hosts and making TCP/IP the mandatory, dominant protocol.

Source: NSFNET history

April 30, 1995 - NSFNET Decommissioned; Internet Commercialized

The NSF shut down the NSFNET backbone it had funded since 1986, handing Internet infrastructure over to commercial Internet Service Providers interconnected at Network Access Points.

Why it mattered: this ended government sponsorship of the backbone, lifted restrictions on commercial use, and opened the door to the private ISPs and e-commerce that define the Internet economy today.

Source: Council on Foreign Relations

Broadband and Mobile Growth (1990s-2010s)

With the backbone handed to commercial providers, the next fight was over how fast an ordinary connection could be - and then, whether it needed a wire at all.

1998 - IPv6 Specification Published

The IETF published RFC 2460, formally specifying IPv6 and its vastly larger address space, anticipating that the 4.3 billion addresses of IPv4 would eventually run out.

Why it mattered: IPv6 gave the Internet a long-term escape route from address exhaustion, though its rollout would take well over a decade to gain real traction.

Source: IETF - RFC 2460

Early 2000s - Broadband Overtakes Dial-Up

Cable and DSL connections spread through homes across North America, Europe, and East Asia, replacing the modem screech of dial-up with always-on, higher-speed access.

Why it mattered: constant, faster connections made streaming media, large downloads, and always-on services practical for ordinary households for the first time.

Source: Pew Research Center

June 6, 2012 - World IPv6 Launch Day

Major ISPs, home networking equipment makers, and web companies including Google and Facebook permanently enabled IPv6 on their products and services in a single coordinated event.

Why it mattered: it marked the point at which IPv6 shifted from an optional experiment to a permanent, production part of the Internet, needed to connect the coming wave of mobile and IoT devices.

Source: Internet Society

2009-2013 - 4G LTE Networks Roll Out

Mobile carriers around the world began deploying 4G LTE networks, offering data speeds that finally approached fixed broadband and made smartphones a practical primary way to access the Internet.

Why it mattered: 4G untethered fast Internet access from the home router, accelerating the shift toward mobile-first, always-connected use of the network.

Source: GSMA

The Contemporary Internet (2010s-Present)

The most recent chapter has been less about new wires and more about what runs over them: who is watching, how fast the connection needs to be, and how much of the traffic is no longer human-generated.

2013 - Global Surveillance Disclosures

Edward Snowden, a former NSA contractor, leaked classified documents revealing the scope of government surveillance programs monitoring Internet traffic worldwide.

Why it mattered: the disclosures triggered a global debate over online privacy and pushed much of the Internet toward default encryption, including the widespread adoption of HTTPS.

Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica

2019 - Commercial 5G Networks Launch

Mobile carriers began switching on commercial 5G networks, promising substantially higher speeds and lower latency than 4G.

Why it mattered: 5G was designed not just for faster phones but to support massive numbers of connected devices, from self-driving cars to industrial sensors, feeding directly into the Internet of Things.

Source: International Telecommunication Union (ITU)

2020 - COVID-19 Pushes Daily Life Onto the Network

The COVID-19 pandemic forced remote work, online schooling, telehealth, and video calling into daily life for billions of people almost overnight, straining ISPs and data centers worldwide.

Why it mattered: it demonstrated, in real time, how much of modern life the Internet could carry when physical alternatives disappeared - and exposed how uneven global broadband access still was.

Source: International Telecommunication Union (ITU)

2022-Present - AI Traffic and Satellite Internet Expansion

Data-center traffic driven by generative AI systems grew rapidly, while low-orbit satellite constellations such as Starlink began delivering broadband to regions that fiber and cable never reached.

Why it mattered: together they are reshaping the Internet's physical demands - more compute-heavy data centers on one end, and coverage finally reaching remote and rural areas on the other.

Source: Internet Society

This timeline was created for educational purposes as part of a self-directed study of Internet history. Dates, figures, and sources have been drawn from institutional archives including the Computer History Museum, the Internet Society, and CERN; each entry links to its original source for further reading.
